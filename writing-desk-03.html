Writing Desk · 03

On the Things
We Become

On backstory, memory, identity, and the unfinished work of becoming.

August 2026

I. The Myth of Backstory

In narrative craft, we often talk about "backstory" as if it were a solid object.

We fill out character sheets with dates, childhood homes, old heartbreaks, and formative injuries, treating these details like geological strata—hardened, unchangeable facts buried beneath the present tense. We like the comfort of a fixed origin story. It gives us a neat equation: because this happened in the past, the character behaves like this today.

But human beings do not carry their histories like dusty museum exhibits.

The past is not a passive archive; it is a fluid, active participant in the present. We do not look back at our earlier lives through clear glass. We look back through the lens of who we have become today. The memory you summoned at twenty-one—raw, urgent, demanding restitution—feels entirely different when recalled at thirty-five, softened by time or sharpened by a new, unrelated grief.

We do not merely retain our history. We negotiate with it.

When a story treats a character's past as a solved equation, it flattens them into a case study. But when it treats the past as a living, unsettled territory— something a person is still trying to make sense of, still trying to forgive, or still trying to outrun— the character suddenly begins to breathe.

II. Loyalty to Dead Versions

One of the quietest tragedies of human life is our fierce, unexamined loyalty to people we no longer are.

We make decisions at eighteen, twenty-two, or thirty, and then spend decades treating those decisions as binding contracts. We inherit a version of ourselves— the protective one, the wounded one, the relentless one, the one who swore never to trust again or never to ask for help—and we defend that identity as if changing it would be a betrayal.

We forget that the person who made those vows was reacting to a world that no longer exists, using tools that were incomplete.

In fiction, this is where the deepest internal friction lives. The real struggle of a character is rarely just overcoming an external obstacle; it is the agonizing process of outgrowing their own self-conception. It is the moment they realize that the armor that kept them safe five years ago has become the very cage keeping them isolated today.

We stay loyal to old grudges because letting them go feels like admitting we suffered for nothing. We stay loyal to old ambitions because abandoning them feels like failing the younger self who wanted them so desperately.

Yet real growth requires a strange kind of mourning. It asks us to allow older versions of ourselves to die gracefully, without holding them against the person we are trying to become.

III. Memory as a Changing Witness

Memory is rarely a neutral bystander. It is a witness whose testimony alters with age.

When we look back on our lives, we do not recall events with cold, archival precision. We curate them. We emphasize the small detail that proves we were right all along; we dim the memory of the conversation where we were careless or cruel; we turn an ambiguous departure into a heroic escape or a tragic abandonment.

We reshape our personal histories so that the story of our lives remains coherent to the person living it today.

This isn't necessarily dishonesty; it is survival. To endure ourselves, we need our past to lead logically to our present. We convert chaos into cause and effect. We take a series of reckless, accidental choices and reframe them as a deliberate journey toward wisdom.

In storytelling, the most compelling characters are not those with pristine, perfectly accurate memories, but those whose memories are subtly compromised by their needs. The way a character remembers an old betrayal reveals far more about who they are today than the betrayal itself ever could.

We do not merely remember who we were. We reshape who we were so we can live with who we are.

IV. The Art of Revision

We often speak of finding ourselves as if identity were a hidden object waiting to be unearthed in the dark—a pristine core buried beneath layers of expectation, fear, and time.

We draft a version of who we are, live in it until it no longer fits, and then face the quiet, terrifying work of crossing out the lines that no longer hold true.

In the hands of a careful writer, a character’s turning point is rarely a sudden illumination. It is a sentence crossed out and rewritten. It is the moment they look at the story they have told themselves about their life— I am the one who was left; I am the one who must fix this; I am incapable of staying—and decide that the draft is no longer accurate.

That is why the most resonant stories do not end with a character arriving at absolute certainty. They end with a character granting themselves permission to be unfinished.

We are not bound to the rough drafts of our past. In the end, perhaps the truest grace we can offer ourselves—and each other—is the willingness to put down our drafts and our projections, to admit that we were wrong, and to remain curious about the person who is still becoming in front of us.
