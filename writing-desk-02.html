Writing Desk · 02

On the Things
We Misread

On projection, silence, intimacy, misunderstanding, and the parts of another person we can never completely know.

August 13, 2026

I. The Stories We Tell Ourselves

The human mind is an aggressive interpreter.

When presented with another person, we rarely permit them to exist as a blank canvas. We are far too restless for that. Faced with incomplete information—a voice that drops an octave, a glance that lingers a second too long, a message left unanswered through an afternoon—we do not wait for clarity. We invent it.

We construct entire interior architectures for people based on a handful of scattered clues. A quiet demeanor becomes coldness; a nervous laugh becomes arrogance; a brief hesitation becomes deliberate withdrawal. We take the smallest, most ambiguous fragments of another person's behavior and weave them into a seamless, convincing story about who they are and what they feel toward us.

What is striking is not just that we do this, but the speed with which we do it. The story is built in a breath. And once the story exists, we begin looking for evidence that will keep it intact. Every subsequent action they take is dragged through the filter of the narrative we have already constructed.

We like to believe we are observing other people. More often than not, we are simply reading our own draft of them.

II. The Problem with Silence

Silence is almost never received as empty space. It is a canvas we paint our deepest anxieties upon.

When a room goes quiet, or when a conversation hits a sudden, unexpected lull, our instinct is rarely to assume that the other person is merely thinking, tired, or absent-minded. Instead, we project into the void. We fill the quiet with whatever story we are most primed to believe about ourselves—our fear of being a burden, our terror of being exposed, or our quiet, lingering suspicion that we are ultimately uninteresting.

In this way, silence becomes a dangerous kind of mirror.

A quiet partner isn't just quiet; they are harboring a secret disappointment. A friend's unread text isn't just a busy day; it is a gradual, deliberate retreat from the friendship. We turn another person's stillness into evidence against them—or against ourselves—long before a single word is actually spoken.

In fiction, as in life, the most devastating misunderstandings rarely come from loud arguments or dramatic lies. They come from the quiet moments where two people sit in the same room, each privately interpreting the other’s silence, building two entirely different, incompatible realities out of nothing at all.

III. When We Get Someone Wrong

There is a distinct, quiet shock in realizing you have completely misread someone you care about.

It usually arrives not in a grand confrontation, but in a small, unscripted moment that refuses to fit into the narrative you’ve built. A sudden admission of fear from someone you thought was invincible. A quiet boundary drawn by someone you assumed would always yield. A tender memory they hold from a shared night that you remembered only as uncomfortable.

In that beat, the illusion shatters. You realize that for months—or years—you were not relating to the human being in front of you. You were relating to a ghost of your own making.

This is where intimacy faces its steepest trial. When the version of someone you invented collides with who they actually are, the ego's first instinct is defence. We want to argue with reality. We want to insist that our version of them was the correct one, that they are being inconsistent, unfair, or untrue to themselves.

To love someone past this point requires a painful act of demolition. It demands that we dismantle our preferred draft of them and do the slow, humbling work of learning who they actually are. Real connection doesn't begin when two people understand each other perfectly; it begins in the aftermath of a misreading, when both people choose recalibration over the safety of their projections.

IV. What We Cannot Know

As writers, we are taught to pursue total clarity—to map every motivation, expose every secret, and lay a character’s inner life bare for the reader. We want to solve our people.

But a human being who is completely explainable ceases to feel human.

The most enduring fiction leaves room for the unmapped territory. It recognizes that even at our most intimate, even after years of shared living and quiet reckonings, there remains an irreducible core in every person that cannot be fully cataloged or known. There are memories they will never share, motives they will never fully understand themselves, and private rooms within their minds where no one else will ever step.

Allowing a character—or a person—to keep that privacy is not a failure of understanding. It is a form of respect.

When we stop demanding that others be entirely legible to us, our relationships change. Misreading ceases to be an insult or a betrayal; it becomes a reminder that the person across from us is vast, evolving, and independent of our imagination.

In the end, perhaps the truest love we can offer another person is not the claim that we know them completely. It is the willingness to put down our drafts and our projections, to admit that we were wrong, and to remain curious about the person who is still becoming in front of us.
