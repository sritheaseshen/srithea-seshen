Writing Desk · 01

On Writing People
Before Plots

On character, choice, intimacy, and the strange inefficiency of being human.

August 12, 2026
01

The Problem with Plots

Plots are wonderfully efficient engines.

Someone leaves. Someone returns. A secret is overheard behind a closed door; a letter arrives three days too late; two strangers collide on a crowded corner and spend the rest of the night talking. Plot loves symmetry. It loves cause and effect. It operates on the clean, satisfying logic of momentum: this happened, and so that must follow.

But real people are terribly inefficient.

We do not move in straight lines toward our desires. We hesitate at open doors. We misread the quietest gestures and assign grand meaning to total accidents. We contradict ourselves within the space of a single breath—demanding honesty from the people we love while hiding the very parts of ourselves that feel too sharp to show.

More than that, we remember unevenly. Put two people in a room during a life-altering moment, and five years later they will offer you two entirely different histories. One remembers the exact words spoken; the other remembers only the light through the window and the sudden, overwhelming desire to leave.

If a story relies too heavily on the machinery of plot, it risks treating people like cargo—moving them neatly from point A to point B to satisfy the itinerary. But a life isn't an itinerary. When we look back on our own histories, we rarely remember the clean sequence of events. We remember how it felt to stand in the middle of them, completely unsure of what to do next.

02

The Choice

There is a quiet temptation in storytelling to believe that trauma or triumph is the whole story. We watch a character survive a loss, fall into a fortune, or endure a betrayal, and we assume the event itself has done the work of defining them.

But event is merely weather.

The far more interesting question is never simply, what happened to this person? It is: what did they decide to do with what happened?

This is the precise threshold where plot surrenders to character. Two people can stand in the wreckage of the exact same storm; one will rebuild the house stone by stone, while the other will pack a single bag and spend the rest of their life moving away from the coast. The storm is identical. The choices can take them in opposite directions.

Character is not a list of traits, virtues, or carefully chosen quirks written down in a notebook before the story begins. Character is the posture a human being adopts when forced into a corner. It lives in the small, agonizing beats between impulse and action: the phone call made or left unmade; the apology swallowed out of pride; the quiet, stubborn refusal to let a bitter moment turn us into bitter people.

We are not merely the sum of our circumstances. We are the sum of what we forfeit, what we protect, and what we forgive—especially when nobody is standing there to applaud the decision.

03

Relationships as Disruption

Left to ourselves, we are remarkably good at maintaining the illusion of stability.

We curate our habits, smooth down our rough edges, and rehearse the stories we tell ourselves about who we are. In solitude, we can convince ourselves that we are patient, reasonable, and composed. We become accustomed to the quiet person we get to be when nobody is asking us to adapt.

Then another person arrives.

A lover, a parent, a child, a friend—sometimes even an adversary. They walk into the neat architecture of our lives, and suddenly the version of ourselves we worked so hard to preserve begins to fracture.

This is the true disruption of intimacy. It isn't just that another person enters our space; it's that they bring a mirror with them. They notice the slight pause before we answer a simple question. They hear the sentence we cut short. They see the fear behind our competence, or the fragility behind our distance.

When that happens, the real conflict of a story is rarely the external conflict alone. It isn't about whether two people will manage to stay in the same room, or win the day, or secure a happy ending.

It is about the cost of being known.

To be truly seen by another human being requires a terrifying kind of surrender. It asks us to give up the protection of our private narratives. It forces us to decide whether we will retreat back into the safety of our solitude, or allow ourselves to be rearranged by love, by grief, or by the slow, difficult work of staying.

04

What Remains

When I sit down to write, I do not begin with the question of what should happen to these people.

I don't look for the dramatic reveal, the sudden twist, or the clean resolution designed to tie a narrative into a tidy bow. Those things have their place, but they are scaffolding.

Instead, I begin with a quieter, far more demanding set of questions: Who are these people when no one is watching? What do they hold onto when the room goes dark? And what happens when someone finally steps into that room, turns on the light, and refuses to leave?

Storytelling, at its most honest, is not an escape from reality; it is an act of attention. It is a refusal to let the noise of events obscure the subtle, monumental shifts happening inside the human heart.

Plots will always offer us momentum—the thrilling, restless drive to find out what comes next. But it is character that gives that momentum a reason to exist. Plot tells us that a threshold was crossed. Character is why we hold our breath as their foot hovers over the line.

In the end, we do not remember stories because of how neatly they were constructed. We remember them because, for a few hundred pages, we were allowed to witness people making the fragile, courageous choice to begin again.
