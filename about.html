The Author

Srithea Seshen

Writer. Researcher. Observer.

I write fiction that privileges human interiority over narrative machinery. My work inhabits the narrow thresholds where memory, myth, and quiet empirical observation converge—examining who we become when no one is watching, and what remains when the room goes dark.

Why I Write

I. The Impulse

I sit down to write because I am a helpless collector of quiet, unexplainable details.

A phrase overheard in a crowded corridor. A melody that lingers in a room long after the music has stopped. The sudden, unscripted posture of a stranger hesitating at a door—a gesture so small it should be forgotten in a breath, yet it lodges in memory like a splinter and refuses to leave.

Long before I ever wrote a line of prose, I noticed that my mind was compulsively, helplessly attentive to real life. I would watch two people in a room and find myself far less interested in the performance they were putting on than in what was happening beneath it—the unspoken tension in a set jaw, the subtle shift in weight away from a table, the heavy silence that fell right after a name was mentioned.

Curiosity, for me, has never been an abstract intellectual exercise. It is a physical sensation. It is the sudden, sharp realization that a detail I have just witnessed holds a secret I haven't yet figured out how to translate.

II. The Two Maps

To make sense of what I observe, I rely on two languages that are often treated as opposites: biochemistry and mythology.

My work in biochemistry gives me a discipline of vision. Science teaches me to look at the world with empirical precision—to respect cellular structures, binding affinities, chemical signals, and the quiet, indifferent laws that govern living systems. It offers the clarity required to see a physical room exactly as it is, without sentimentality, recognizing that even our deepest emotions are anchored in physical reality.

Mythology offers something else entirely: scale.

An ancient myth is not an escape from reality. It is an archetypal map of recurring human experience—a recognition that we are not the first people to stand in the wreckage of a choice, to misread someone we love, or to feel the terror of beginning again. Mythology gives us the ancient shapes that lie beneath our modern, ordinary lives.

Where biochemistry observes how the system functions, mythology measures the weight of what it feels like to live inside it. I find I cannot write about human beings using only one map. I need the precision to see the cell, and the ancient pattern to understand why it aches.

III. The Raw Material

Where science and mythology collide is in real life—in real stories, music, and everyday human exchange.

People are endlessly, beautifully inefficient. We do not move in straight lines toward our desires. We contradict ourselves in the space of a single breath, demanding honesty from others while hiding the very parts of ourselves that feel too sharp to show.

And nowhere is this clearer than in conversation and music.

We rarely say what we mean. Real dialogue—like a piece of music—is defined as much by its pauses, its dissonance, and its underlying rhythms as by the notes played out loud. It is a quiet game of translation and misdirection: partial truths, deliberate silences, and small gestures designed to protect the ego. I listen to real stories not just for the events that took place, but for the underlying frequency: the fear behind competence, the fragility behind distance, the sudden admission buried inside a joke.

We are walking libraries of everything we have inherited, misremembered, and projected onto one another. We live in the tension between who we think we are and who we actually reveal ourselves to be when the room goes dark.

IV. What Remains

Non-fiction can record what happened. Science can explain how it functioned. History can document when it occurred.

But fiction is the only space we have that allows us to step inside the interiority of another human being.

It is a refusal to let the noise of events obscure the subtle, monumental shifts happening inside the human heart. We construct stories not to solve people, but to pay attention to them—to grant them their privacy, their contradictions, and their irreducible human mystery.

In the end, I write fiction because I believe that character is posture, that plot is merely weather, and that love, memory, and the courage to begin again are the only things that truly remain.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do you begin with plot or character?

A person or a situation—provoked by a song or a piece of music. Sometimes a quote, a text. Sometimes it's a contradiction they can't quite explain. I spend a long time listening before I ask them to carry a story.

Why do memory and mythology appear so often in your work?

My background in biochemistry taught me that systems are constantly adapting, breaking down, and re-regulating themselves. In human life, memory and mythology are how we regulate the emotional heart.

We don't remember our lives with cold objectivity; memory is an active, changing witness. And when our personal memories feel too chaotic or overwhelming, mythology gives us a container—a reminder that people have walked through this exact dark room before. I bring both into my fiction because I'm fascinated by how we use ancient stories to make sense of ordinary, modern heartbreaks.

Does being a scientist change the way you write?

It makes me a more disciplined observer. Science teaches you to honor cause and effect without judging the outcome. When I write fiction, that background keeps me honest—it stops me from forcing a plot and forces me to pay attention to how human beings actually behave when the pressure is on.

Are your characters based on real people?

Mostly, yes—but rarely in a straight line.

My characters almost always begin with real people: a specific posture, a rhythm of speech, a way of holding a silence, or an emotional contradiction I've witnessed in real life. But once a character enters a story, they cease to be a transcript. Fiction acts like a prism. You take the truth of someone you know, combine it with the voice or grief of someone else, and place them in a situation they never actually faced. They start as people I've known, but they earn their own independent lives on the page.

Why romance?

Because nothing reveals who we are faster than the desire to connect with someone else. Romance is the room where we are forced to put down our drafts, admit who we actually are, and take the risk of being known.

Why do your stories feel quiet?

Because the metaphysical requires quiet. You can't hear an echo in a room full of shouting.

My stories are quiet because I am interested in the invisible architecture of human life—the unsaid truths, the weight of memory, and the unseen connections that pull people together. When you quiet the external noise, an ordinary conversation in a small room suddenly begins to shimmer.

Do you believe happy endings exist?

I believe in happy endings, but I define them differently. A happy ending isn't the absence of future trouble; it's the presence of truth, choice, and the courage to stay open to the person becoming in front of you.

What makes you stop writing?

When the story starts trying to answer questions it hasn't earned the right to answer yet. I step away to let the silence do its job, and I only come back when I'm ready to listen again.

What are you trying to leave readers with?

The feeling that they have just stepped out of a very quiet room where something monumental just shifted. I want to leave them with the understanding that the most ordinary human moments are actually carrying the weight of the world.

Welcome to the desk.

In the end, we don't read or write to solve one another. We do it to pay attention. To the quiet shifts, the unsaid truths, and the stories we are still revising.
